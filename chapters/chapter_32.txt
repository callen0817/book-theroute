Chapter 32 — A Shadow from the Past
Seasons continue their quiet turning in the valley.
Children grow in ways that are almost impossible to notice day by day. Their legs lengthen, their voices deepen, and their curiosity stretches farther than the paths we have worn into the grass. What once felt like a fragile settlement now hums with movement and laughter.
Life settles into rhythms we begin to trust.
Gardens expand along the riverbanks. New shelters rise where young families begin lives of their own. The valley carries the sound of work, learning, and the steady patience of people who have finally found a place worth tending.
For a long time, it feels as though the world has softened.
But even in peaceful places, the past sometimes arrives quietly.
At first, we notice nothing unusual about Amara. She moves through her days as she always has—steady, attentive, present in every small moment that keeps a life running smoothly. She has always possessed a quiet strength that does not draw attention to itself.
Then small changes begin.
They are easy to dismiss at first.
She grows tired more quickly when carrying water from the river. Sometimes her hands tremble slightly when she lifts a basket of roots from the garden. Ash tells her to rest more, and she laughs softly, brushing away his concern.
But the tiredness does not pass.
Weeks stretch into months.
Her strength fades slowly, like a flame burning lower without anyone noticing the moment it began to dim.
Ash watches closely.
He has spent too much of his life studying danger to ignore small changes. Yet this is a danger he cannot understand. There is no wound, no fever, no sign of illness that explains what is happening.
One evening we sit beside the river while the children play along the banks.
Ash stares into the moving water for a long time before speaking.
“Have you ever seen something like this?” he asks quietly.
I shake my head.
The world has taught us many lessons, but this is not one we know.
The answer reveals itself slowly, through memory more than discovery.
Years ago, when we first mapped the lands surrounding this valley, we heard stories about the feeder regions beyond the mountains. Small settlements that survived the harshest years in ways most people preferred not to speak about.
Places where hunger forced terrible choices.
Sometimes those choices were not even understood by the people making them.
Amara had once spoken softly of her birth in those distant, shadow-bound valleys. Whatever had happened there in the years of famine, she carried without knowing. Her mind held only the quiet innocence of childhood; it was her origins alone that gave Ash and me more understanding than she ever possessed. She had lived in peace, shielded by her own unknowing.
But the body remembers what the mind does not always know.
In rare cases, people who unknowingly consumed human flesh for long periods carried something within them afterward. Something that moved slowly and silently through the body, sometimes taking years before its effects became visible.
When the realization settles between us, neither of us speaks for a long time.
Ash listens carefully as I explain the fragments of knowledge we have gathered over the years.
There is no anger in his face.
Only a deep, quiet sadness.
“It wasn’t her fault,” he says after a long silence.
And he is right.
She had been a child then, living in a world shaped by survival more than understanding. Whatever happened in those valleys was never her choice.
She simply carried its shadow without knowing.
Ash stays beside her as the days grow heavier.
The valley continues around us—wind in the grass, water slipping through the stones, children running through the fields. Life moves forward the way it always does, even as one life slowly begins to fade.
When the end finally comes, it is quiet.
Ash buries Amara beneath a young tree near the river, where sunlight reaches the ground in long golden stretches during the afternoon. His children stand beside him, uncertain how to measure something so final.
No words are needed.
Grief settles over him like a long winter.
But Ash does not leave.
Not yet.
His children are still growing, still learning the world beneath their feet. They follow him constantly now, asking questions, studying his movements, trying to understand the careful patience he brings to every task.
Ash answers them the only way he knows how.
By showing them.
He teaches them how to move through the forest without disturbing the animals. How to watch the sky for weather. How to read the quiet signs that the earth offers to those who pay attention.
Sometimes he says very little.
But they learn.
Years pass this way.
The children grow stronger, more confident, more curious about the land beyond the valley. I watch them begin to wander farther during their explorations, their feet slowly discovering the same restless pull that once guided all of us.
Ash sees it too.
One evening we stand together along the ridge above the valley.
His children move across the fields below, following a narrow trail toward the forest’s edge.
“They’re starting to hear it,” Ash says quietly.
“The road,” I reply.
He nods.
The horizon stretches endlessly beyond the hills, the old paths still threading through the world the way they always have.
Ash watches the distant line where land meets sky.
For the first time since Amara died, I see something return to his expression.
Not happiness.
But recognition.
The road is still there.
Waiting.
